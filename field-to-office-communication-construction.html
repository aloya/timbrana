Field-to-office communication

Better construction communication is not more messages. It is clearer handoffs.

The field and office already communicate constantly. The harder problem is keeping the reason, decision and responsibility attached as information moves between them.

For general contractorsUpdated September 28, 2026

Why field-to-office communication breaks down

A superintendent may see a condition in the field, call the project manager, text a photo and mention it again in the daily log. The information exists, but it is spread across several places. When the project manager later needs to price it, ask the owner or send an RFI, the team has to reconstruct the original context.

The problem is usually not a lack of communication. It is the gap between communication and project responsibility.

What should move from the field to the office?

Conditions needing a decisionAnything that stops work, changes sequencing or cannot be resolved by normal field coordination.
CommitmentsReturn dates, promised answers, delivery dates and other statements that should become visible project expectations.
Scope questionsConditions that may require an RFI, change order or confirmation of who owns the work.
Owner involvementSelections, questions, approvals and requests that need a client response without exposing internal discussion.
Schedule impactsNo-shows, late deliveries, inspection outcomes or incomplete predecessor work that changes what can happen next.

What should stay attached to the information?

A handoff is much stronger when the office receives more than a short description. The field context should travel with the item.

OriginWhere did the item come from: a daily log, call, walkthrough, inspection, owner request or field observation?
ReasonWhy does it matter to the project? What work, area or decision is affected?
EvidencePhotos, notes or supporting information that prevent a second round of explanation.
Current responsibilityWho has the next move now — superintendent, PM, trade partner, design team or owner?
Next actionWhat is actually expected: answer, return, pricing, correction, inspection, approval or another specific action?
The handoff should answer “who has it now?”

A complete history is useful, but the current responsibility is what keeps the project moving.

Use the right workflow instead of one communication bucket

A call, email or text is often how an item begins. It should not automatically remain a communication item forever. Once the team understands what the message means, move it into the workflow that owns the next step.

A field question may become an RFI. A client request may become an owner question. A condition with cost impact may become a change order. A subcontractor return commitment may become a scheduled event. The original communication should remain connected, but the project should not depend on someone remembering to reread it.

How the office can respond without creating duplicate work

The project manager should be able to act on the field item without asking the superintendent to create it again in a different form. That means the office can review, clarify and move the item while preserving the original field context.

This is especially important when the same condition affects several workflows. For example, a field issue can lead to an RFI and later a change order. Those are different project records, but they should still tell one understandable story.

A simple field-to-office communication test

For any important item, someone who was not part of the original conversation should be able to answer these questions:

What happened?The original condition or communication is understandable.
Why does it matter?The affected work, decision or schedule consequence is clear.
Who has it?The current responsible party is visible.
What happens next?The expected next action is specific enough to follow.

That is the operating layer Timbrana describes as Project Awareness: not simply storing messages, but keeping the project meaning of those messages visible as responsibility changes.
